# Street Lab visual system

Use this reference to analyze or design the visual language. Treat it as a flexible system, not a requirement to copy a particular poster.

## 1. Visual signature

The style combines five layers:

1. extreme action-camera perspective;
2. a premium 3D mascot, character, or product hero;
3. architecture forming a radial frame around open sky;
4. a bold two-color brand system;
5. editorial campaign graphics applied as a separate surface layer.

The result should feel like a designer-toy campaign, streetwear advertisement, action-film key art, and experimental magazine cover sharing one canvas.

## 2. Composition grammar

### Default frame

- Portrait ratio: 3:4. Accept 4:5 or 9:16 when required.
- Camera: ground-level upward view, 14–16 mm fisheye or strong curvilinear ultrawide.
- Hero: centered slightly below the optical center so the sky remains visible.
- Background: tall buildings or environmental forms curve inward and describe an oval or circular opening.
- Foreground: one or two cropped elements enter from opposite lower corners, producing diagonal tension.
- Title: occupies approximately the bottom 25–32 percent, overlapping debris or the subject edge but not the face.
- Utility labels: remain sparse and live near the outer frame.

### Depth bands

1. **Near:** a hand, sole, wheel, product edge, ribbon, tool, paw, or other meaningful element becomes dramatically oversized.
2. **Hero:** face, body, or product remains sharp and identifiable.
3. **Frame:** architecture, signage, sky, clouds, and light create scale and radial pull.

At least one foreground shape should break the frame. Keep the hero silhouette readable; distortion belongs mainly to the near band and outer architecture.

### Motion

Use perspective first, then support it with flying grit, dust, small fragments, paint droplets, fabric, laces, or light streaks. Avoid filling the entire poster with equally intense particles. Motion should travel along one main diagonal or radiate from the lower center.

## 3. Color system

### Default palette

| Role | Color | Approximate value |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | saturated cobalt blue | `#0754A6` |
| Accent | vivid golden yellow | `#F7C600` |
| Dark anchor | charcoal black | `#101318` |
| Light anchor | warm white | `#F4F1E8` |
| Heat | amber orange | `#D97820` |
| Sky | clear cyan blue | `#6DB6E8` |

Aim for roughly 55–65% dark/blue structure, 15–25% light/sky, 8–15% yellow accents, and a small amount of warm orange. These are visual balance guides, not pixel quotas.

When adapting a brand palette, keep the same functional roles: one deep field color, one high-energy accent, one neutral dark, one neutral light, and an optional warm highlight. Maintain clear luminance separation.

## 4. Rendering and materials

- Medium: polished stylized 3D CGI or realistic product CGI.
- Character finish: designer-toy proportions with expressive eyes and tactile skin, fur, hair, or fabric.
- Materials: visible weave, rubber grooves, worn concrete, glass reflections, painted metal, and restrained surface imperfections.
- Lighting: bright daylight or glow at the center; darker vignetting at the perimeter; warm practical lights in windows; thin rim light around the hero.
- Focus: sharp hero and meaningful foreground texture, with gentle motion or lens blur at the extreme edges.
- Finish: cinematic HDR without plastic highlights, crushed shadows, or uniform oversharpening.

## 5. Graphic surface layer

The graphic layer should feel art-directed rather than pasted on:

- T1: condensed uppercase block title, usually white, large, distressed, and partially cropped.
- T1 accent: yellow hand-painted brush word, underline, or diagonal slash.
- T2: short promise in a clean sans-serif, two or three lines at most.
- T3: date, place, coordinates, product detail, or call to action in compact modules.
- T4: tiny technical labels, reticles, thin rules, arrows, or numbering near the edges.
- Texture: dry-brush blue/yellow strokes, ink speckle, dust, and localized print wear.

Use no more than two dominant display type personalities: one heavy condensed face and one brush/marker accent. Use a neutral grotesk for small information.

## 6. Adaptation rules

- **Product:** use the product itself as the hero; project a corner, wheel, cap, cable, or functional feature toward the lens.
- **Vehicle:** project a tire or headlight; let roads and buildings form the radial frame.
- **Animal or mascot:** project a paw, wing, tail, accessory, or tool rather than automatically adding a human hand.
- **Fashion:** use a shoe, sleeve, bag, or fabric fold for foreground thrust.
- **Architecture/event:** use a stage, doorway, sculpture, sign, or pathway as the foreground anchor and scale cue.

Avoid literal duplication of source characters, logos, copy, and proprietary product shapes when the request is only for style extraction.

## 7. Failure patterns

- A wide-angle image without an oversized near element feels merely architectural.
- A large hand or shoe with no story connection feels gimmicky.
- Equal detail everywhere destroys the central hierarchy.
- Too many labels turn the edge system into interface clutter.
- Generating several long text blocks inside the image produces gibberish.
- Using blue and yellow at equal brightness everywhere removes contrast.
- Excessive dirt and paint obscure premium material rendering.
